# Reflection

## Instructions

Reflections are a metcognitive activity where you are encouraged to think about your own thinking. It helps you build a strong understanding of your own learning. A good learner not only "knows what they know", but they "know what they don't know", too. Learning to reflect takes practice, but if your goal is to become a self-directed learner where you can teach yourself things, reflection is imperative.

- Now that you've completed the assignment, think about what you did and share your thoughts. What did you learn? What confuses you? Where did you struggle? Where might you need more practice?
- A good reflection is: **specific as possible**,  **uses the terminology of the problem domain** (what was learned in class / through readings), and **is actionable** (you can pursue next steps, or be aided in the pursuit). That last part is what will make you a self-directed learner.
- Flex your recall muscles. You might have to review class notes / assigned readings to write your reflection and get the terminology correct.
- Your reflection is for **you**. Yes I make you write them and I read them, but you are merely practicing to become a better self-directed learner. If you read your reflection 1 week later, does what you wrote advance your learning?

Examples:

- **Poor Reflection:**  "I don't understand loops."   
**Better Reflection:** "I don't undersand how the while loop exits."   
**Best Reflection:** "I struggle writing the proper exit conditions on a while loop." It's actionable: You can practice this, google it, ask Chat GPT to explain it, etc. 
-  **Poor Reflection** "I learned loops."   
**Better Reflection** "I learned how to write while loops and their difference from for loops."   
**Best Reflection** "I learned when to use while vs for loops. While loops are for sentiel-controlled values (waiting for a condition to occur), vs for loops are for iterating over collections of fixed values."

In this assignment I learned when to use each way of sending data with requests: the Azure endpoints take a form (data=), the AI endpoints take a JSON body (json=), and model settings like model and reasoning_effort go in the query string (params=). Sending the wrong one gives a 4xx status, and raise_for_status() turns that into an exception at the line that caused it. Without it, the error comes back as a normal-looking dict and fails later as a confusing KeyError. A 401 means a bad or missing API key, a 429 means I hit the 150-request daily limit, and a 5xx means the server had a problem. I learned that caching means checking whether the cache file exists, and if not, calling the API once per review and saving the raw response with json.dump. This protects the rate limit and makes debugging fast, because the transform steps (sentences_table, opinions_table) only reshape saved JSON and never touch the network. That is the difference between extract and transform. I also learned why two AI services are combined: opinion mining handled T3, where one sentence has two opposite opinions (love the seating, music too loud), but it missed Pete, Tally and Otto because they have no assessments attached, and entity recognition found them. The hardest part was opinions_table. json_normalize with record_path, meta and record_prefix worked for sentences_table, but opinions_table was too deep because each target points to its assessments by a ref string, so I needed nested for loops and int(ref.split("/")[-1]) to find the right assessment in the same sentence. I still mix up what meta copies from the parent document versus what record_prefix renames (like sentence_text), and I don't fully understand why the decision model's request must be byte-for-byte identical to hit the portal's cache. I also learned the idea behind retrieval-augmented generation: pandas computes the counts and the engineered net column in summarize_items, and the language model only writes from those facts, because LLMs are bad at counting and good at writing.
